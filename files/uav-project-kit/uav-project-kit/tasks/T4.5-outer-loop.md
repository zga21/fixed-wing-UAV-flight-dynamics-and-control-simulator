## Background

The outermost loop, at 10 Hz. It takes "fly at 100 m and 25 m/s" and turns it
into attitude commands.

The naive approach — elevator controls altitude, throttle controls speed — works
badly, because the two are physically coupled. Pull the nose up and you gain
height but lose speed. Add throttle and you gain speed but also climb.

Total Energy Control handles this properly by changing coordinates. Think in
terms of the aircraft's energy instead: **throttle controls the total amount of
energy** (kinetic plus potential), while **elevator controls how that energy is
distributed** between speed and height. The two commands then act on genuinely
independent quantities, and the loops stop fighting.

## Acceptance criteria

- [ ] Altitude step of 50 m: captured within 20 s, overshoot under 10%,
      airspeed deviation under 3 m/s throughout
- [ ] Airspeed step of 5 m/s: achieved within 10 s, altitude deviation under 5 m
- [ ] **Decoupling demonstrated.** Command an altitude change and an airspeed
      change simultaneously; both are achieved without the loops fighting. Save
      the plot
- [ ] Heading change of 90°: completed within 15 s, altitude held within 10 m
- [ ] Full mission (climb, turn, cruise, descend, turn, land approach) completes
      with no envelope violations
- [ ] All three loops run at their specified rates — assert the sub-sampling
      counters actually work
- [ ] `gains` vector exposes every tunable parameter across all three loops,
      with sensible bounds, ready for Phase 7

## Notes

Do not skimp here to get to the nonlinear controller faster. The single most
common way a controller-comparison study fails review is that the baseline was
obviously under-developed. Your Phase 6 result is only as strong as the thing it
beats — and Phase 7 will tune both with the same optimiser and budget, so a
weak baseline will simply make your comparison look unfair rather than
impressive.
