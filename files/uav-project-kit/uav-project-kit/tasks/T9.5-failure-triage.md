## Background

You now have several hundred failed cases. Before reporting any of them, you have
to answer one question: are these **real control failures**, or **simulator
bugs**?

They are not the same thing, and the difference matters enormously. A control
failure is a result. A simulator bug that produces NaN in extreme conditions is a
defect that invalidates part of your dataset.

You will almost certainly find at least one bug this way. Everybody does. Finding
it now, deliberately, is much better than a reviewer finding it later.

## Acceptance criteria

- [ ] Every failure classified
- [ ] The ten worst cases re-run at full logging rate and exported
- [ ] **Each of the ten inspected visually**, with a one-line note on what
      happened: "stalled during the climb segment in severe turbulence",
      "aileron rate-saturated during the turn and never recovered"
- [ ] All NUMERICAL failures investigated. If a simulator bug is found: fix it,
      add a gate test that catches it, and **re-run the affected campaign**.
      Document this in the report — finding and fixing it is a strength, not a
      weakness
- [ ] Failure modes summarised: what fraction of failures share a root cause
- [ ] The two or three most illustrative trajectories exported for the website
- [ ] `docs/failure_analysis.md` written

## Notes

Be honest about what you find. If 5% of your cases failed for numerical reasons
and you excluded them, say so explicitly and state how the headline number
changes with and without them. Quietly dropping cases is the kind of thing that
destroys trust in an otherwise good study.

Conversely, a report that says "we found a guard condition that produced NaN at
airspeeds below 11 m/s in 0.3% of cases, fixed it, added test X, and re-ran the
affected 3,000 cases" reads as a project that was done properly.
