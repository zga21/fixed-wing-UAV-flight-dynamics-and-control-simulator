## Background

The engine pushes the aircraft forward. For a propeller, the thrust depends on
how fast the propeller is turning *and* how fast the aircraft is already moving:
a propeller pushing against still air produces much more thrust than one already
moving through the air at 40 m/s. That speed dependence is what makes trim
interesting — it is why the throttle needed for level flight changes with
airspeed.

The model below is a simple momentum-theory approximation. It is adequate,
standard for small UAV work, and easy to defend in the report. Keep it simple
here; propulsion is not the research question.

## What to implement

```python
def propeller_thrust(delta_t: float, V: float, rho: float,
                     prop: PropulsionConfig) -> float:
    """
    Momentum-theory propeller thrust.

        T = 0.5 * rho * S_prop * C_prop * ((k_motor * delta_t)^2 - V^2)

    The V^2 term is why thrust falls off with airspeed. Clamped at zero:
    a windmilling propeller producing negative thrust is real, but it is an
    unnecessary complication here and it destabilises the trim solver.

    Args:
        delta_t: throttle, 0 to 1 (clipped)
        V: true airspeed, m/s
        rho: air density, kg/m^3
        prop: S_prop, C_prop, k_motor from config
    Returns: thrust magnitude, N, along +x_b
    """

def propulsion_forces_moments(delta_t, air, prop
```

## Acceptance criteria

- [ ] `delta_t = 0` produces zero thrust at every airspeed
- [ ] Thrust increases monotonically with `delta_t` at fixed `V`
- [ ] Thrust **decreases** monotonically with `V` at fixed `delta_t`
- [ ] Thrust is never negative (clamped)
- [ ] `delta_t` outside `[0, 1]` is clipped, not an error
- [ ] Static thrust at full throttle is a plausible fraction of aircraft weight —
      for a small agile UAV expect roughly 0.5 to 1.5 times weight. If you get
      0.02 or 50, check `k_motor`
- [ ] Thrust scales with density: halving `rho` halves thrust

## Notes

The thrust-to-weight sanity check is the useful one. It determines whether your
aircraft can climb at all, and an implausible value here will show up in Phase 3
as a trim solver that fails to converge at climb angles — at which point you
will suspect the trim solver rather than the propulsion constants.
