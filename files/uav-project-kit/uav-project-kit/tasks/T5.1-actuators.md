## Background

So far the control surfaces have been magic: commanded 10 degrees, instantly at
10 degrees. Real servos are slow, they cannot move faster than a certain rate,
and they cannot go past their mechanical stops.

All three limits matter, and the **order they are applied in** matters more than
people expect. Lag first, then rate limit, then position saturation. Apply the
rate limit after saturation and it silently does nothing, because the saturated
signal is already smooth.

Rate limiting is the one that hurts controllers most. A controller that commands
fast corrections will find them delivered late and attenuated, which adds phase
lag exactly where the loop is most sensitive to it. This is where aggressive
Phase 4 gains start to show their cost.

Expose `is_saturated` and `is_rate_limited` — T4.6's `saturation_fraction`
metric needs them, and Phase 7's objective penalises saturation directly.

## Acceptance criteria

- [ ] Small step: response matches `1 - exp(-t/tau)` to 1%
- [ ] Large step: takes exactly `delta / rate_max` seconds to traverse. Assert
      the time, not just the endpoint
- [ ] Command beyond the mechanical limit: output clamps, and `is_saturated`
      is True
- [ ] **Order check.** With a large step, the response must be rate-limited
      (a straight ramp), not exponential. If you see an exponential, the rate
      limit is being applied in the wrong place
- [ ] `reset()` returns to the initial position and clears internal state
- [ ] Deterministic, no randomness
- [ ] Actuators integrate at the simulation rate (500 Hz), not the controller
      rate — they are part of the plant, not the controller
